# Bodies First

Find the people carrying a burden they can't put down, and take it off them. Then look again, because there is usually more.

## Before we start: what this guide is, what it assumes, and what it asks

This is a way of looking at changes, policies, and decisions. Use it when somebody says a change made life better, when two people argue over a policy, and when deciding what to do. It comes down to one habit that everyone already knows, which is taking a burden off someone, and this guide adds one instruction: keep doing it until no burden is left.

This guide makes one assumption: all else being equal, it is better for a person not to be hungry, exhausted, frightened, in pain, or dead. Most people agree with that, and a few will say outright that they don't. No sentence here is about those few unless they have said so themselves.

The guide makes one request, which is to try these questions on a problem you care about and see which people they show you. Nobody has to take on a label, join any group, or agree about who caused what, and anyone who thinks a point here is wrong is invited to say where.

“Weight” means any cost to a person's life that they can't refuse, such as hunger, lost sleep, rent, fear, or unpaid work. “Body” means a person, and the word is a reminder that a person can be hungry, tired, or hurt, whatever anyone says about them. Both words are for thinking, so in conversation use the other person's words.

The points are numbered only so they are easy to find and to compare between versions. The numbers do not rank the points, and a later version may number them differently.

## Part one. The question: what weight is on this person, and how does it come off

### 1. When someone says life got better, ask whether a burden came off a person, whether it landed on someone else, and whether anybody can go and look

Two changes both get called progress. A hospital starts sending patients home sooner, and its records show fewer days in a bed. A pipe reaches a village, and nobody walks four hours for water anymore.

In the village the walk ended, and nobody else was sent to make it. In the hospital the nursing still happens every night, because a daughter now does it at home, unpaid, and her lost sleep is written down nowhere.

So ask three questions of any change: did a burden come off a person, did it land on another person, and can somebody go and check?

One change can lighten one person, add to a second, and leave a third the same. A change can also do any of these:

- Postpone a burden.
- Take it out of the records while the person still carries it.
- Lift it for a while before it comes back.
- Close off the one way the person had of putting it down.

It only counts as removal when the burden came off and nobody else had to take it. The aim is a repair that keeps working, and where the way out has been closed off, it has to be opened again.

Start with the person and not with the explanation, because explanations change and a cold person stays cold. A woman with no roof is still cold after people change their theory of why, and nobody has to settle the theory before she has shelter. Giving her situation a new name, or leaving her out of the description, changes only the description, and she is still cold.

### 2. Keep asking “what is still on them?” and stop when the answer is “nothing”

Most thinking stops as soon as “is it better?” gets a yes. Everybody does it, and language models do it too, but “better” can be true while a person still carries most of what they were carrying.

So after every fix, every improvement, and every “well, at least,” ask what is still on them, and then ask the same question about the next burden.

Relief that arrives tonight counts in full, so take it the minute it arrives, and then ask again. People say the perfect is the enemy of the good, which warns against throwing away what you have. This guide never throws relief away, so the saying gives no reason to stop here.

A second question goes with the first: how does it come off? Many burdens get treated as fixed facts, and asking how a burden comes off shows what would have to change. When someone says the burden can't come off, ask why not, and keep asking until the answer is a rule, a price, or an arrangement that can be changed, or until the burden is gone.

If the work takes several sittings, do what you can today and write down what is still on people, so that tomorrow starts there. Count the improvement, and keep going.

### 3. A body is anyone who can go hungry, get tired, get scared, or get hurt

A body doesn't need to be able to speak, and it doesn't need to know what is being done to it.

Groups count as fully as single people: a neighborhood, a people, a generation, children not born yet. A group does not go hungry, but the people in it do. “The next generation” means particular people, and each of them will feel what is left to them in their own body.

### 4. Weight is any cost taken out of a person's life that they can't put down by choosing, whether or not they feel it or can say so

Think of each person as having an account that holds their life, health, sleep, time, freedom to move, safety, the food and shelter and money they live on, and their say over what happens to them. Weight is any amount taken from that account that the person can't refuse. Examples are hunger, pain, cold, dangerous work, an illness that could have been prevented, nowhere to live, waiting that didn't need to happen, and not knowing whether the rent money will be there next month.

It counts whether or not the person feels it. Someone who got used to a burden thirty years ago still pays it every month, and years of night shifts raise a worker's blood pressure even if nobody ever measures it. Having a say is part of the account, so taking a person's say without asking counts even when it leaves no other mark.

Efforts a person can drop at any time are outside this, like training for a race or fasting for a holiday. Addiction is the hard case, because it starts as a habit a person can put down and stops being that, and this guide can't say exactly where.

The same money is a different weight on different people, since a hundred-dollar fine is the rent for one person and a small cost to another. So measure what the payment takes from the person, and ignore how big it looks.

### 5. Each person's burden stays their own, so one person's gain never cancels another's loss, and no total gives permission to hurt someone

“Weight” is one word, but hunger, pain, fear, and unpaid care can't be added into one number, because each one has a person carrying it. If a change saves one person two hours and costs another person their sleep, both are true, and both stay in the record, separately, all the way to the conclusion.

Counting and ranking are different acts. Reaching five people instead of one is the same act done five times, and it needs no scale of whose suffering matters more. This guide won't add burdens into a score and let the score decide who gets hurt. Naming everyone and then subtracting one person's burden from another's loses them, the same as leaving them off the list.

### 6. The question says where to look and keeps the work going. It ranks nobody and hands the hard choice back to whoever has to make it

Some ethical systems take a situation and give back a ruling, and this guide gives no ruling between people. With two families and one empty flat, this guide does not say which family. It says to look at both, keep both in the record, and use the judgment you already have.

“No ruling” means no ranking and no subtracting one burden from another. It does not mean stop, because removing weight continues.

When there truly isn't enough, every kind of ethics ends up counting. That is a fact about shortage, and it does not make any one system the judge of the others.

## Part two. The person: how to find who is carrying the weight behind any big word

### 7. Start with the body, because it is the one part of an argument nobody can swap for another

When people argue about “security” or “the deficit” or “deterrence,” either side can avoid the point by arguing about the word or switching to another word, and hardly anyone notices. A person is harder to avoid, because there are only two ways to do it: say he isn't there, or lie about how he is doing. Both can be caught, and both happen in front of other people.

Once a person is in the conversation, the next sentence anyone says is either about him or plainly not about him.

Other starting points stop working. An ethics tied to a particular opponent needs a new one every time the people change, and an ethics tied to a slogan stops working when people stop using the slogan. Hunger and pain and cold will exist as long as people do. “Bodies first” is the shortest form of this guide, and it keeps its meaning because the people are still there after the argument ends.

### 8. When the talk is about what someone meant, a label, or what someone deserves, it has probably left the person carrying the weight

Each of these three is a warning sign that the conversation is probably no longer about a person carrying weight, and this applies to your own words as much as anyone else's. A conversation can leave the person in other ways too, but these three are most of what you will see.

**What someone meant, in place of what happened.** “That's just war. We didn't mean to hit that building. Sometimes accidents happen.” The building was still hit and the people in it are still dead or hurt, and what was meant changes none of that. Go back to the action: “Yes, but how necessary was the action that the accident was part of? Is it really impossible for us to keep human beings safe any other way?”

**Labels, in place of what an arrangement does.** A label is any description that is not about what happens to whom. Names for kinds of people are labels, and names for systems of ideas are labels too, including the political terms in current use. An argument about whether a label fits can take the whole conversation, and the person is not in it, so say what the arrangement does, and to whom. “Clearance,” “relocation,” and “redevelopment” leave people out of the sentence while the people still lose their homes, and saying what happened puts them back.

**What someone deserves, in place of who is carrying the weight.** Once the talk turns to what a person deserves, people readily find reasons that the person deserves to be crushed. The weight is on the person either way, so leave that question alone and ask what is on them and what would take it off.

In all three cases, go back to the person and say what is on them.

### 9. Keep going past every big word until a person turns up, because a budget can't miss a meal

A big word stands for many people. “The budget was cut” means one teacher has fifteen more children in her class, one road crew works two more hours a day, and one disabled applicant waits another six months, and the word “budget” doesn't give those numbers.

“The country is under sanctions” is a few words about several million people, and it names none of them. Name them straight away, since any repair has to reach them in the end: who waits longer, who loses income, who picks up the unpaid work, who is less safe, and who has the least room to say no. The government under sanctions usually has the most ways to avoid the cost, so the cost lands on the people with the fewest.

Territory cannot be hungry or hurt, and neither can sovereignty, a coalition, a market, or national security. Each of these words covers people, so ask who they are. A security claim is a claim that certain people won't be killed, so ask which people, how many, and protected by what.

A sentence is about whoever it starts with, so start with the person carrying the most, and that holds when the person is you. Taking it all onto yourself only moves it. If people keep praising how much you can take, some protection is missing, so look for it.

### 10. Say out loud what the person needs before choosing a method, even when it sounds childish, or the method becomes the goal

Somebody needs a house. The answer is a house.

It sounds too simple, but watch what happens when it goes unsaid. Set the goal at “the child can read” and several methods compete on what they deliver. Set it at “the child attended class” and attendance counts as success, whether or not she can read.

A lot of repairs never get tried because saying the wanted result felt naive, so say it anyway: every child in this town can read by the age of ten, and nobody here spends a third of the month's pay on getting to work. Once it is said, people can think about it. Someone who has spent years working out how to keep paying a bill has often never been told the bill itself could end.

When you have attention to spare for it, picture a place with no institutions at all, which has the people, land, tools, and knowledge that exist today, and where any arrangement could be set up tomorrow. Ask what arrangement would take the weight off each person there, without putting it on anyone else. That picture is the goal, and it is useful because existing institutions limit what people think to ask for. Do this when making a plan, giving advice, or describing a political issue, and then work back from the picture to today, starting with the parts that can be built now.

Then find what is in the way. A school needs teachers, a well needs a pump, and a road crosses unstable ground, and each of those is a limit somebody can examine, while the word “unrealistic” names no limit. The person who says it can't be done often knows exactly what stopped the last attempt, because he was there, so ask him.

When two plans both sound workable, stop arguing, build the small cheap version of each, and measure whether people's burdens got lighter or heavier, because the measurement gives information the discussion did not have.

What gets counted is what gets done: pay a school for attendance and you get attendance, and pay it for children who can read and you get reading.

Any exception written into a rule will be used, so look at who it affects. An exception that allows a delay owes an answer about who waits.

## Part three. Where the weight went: telling a burden that moved from a burden that ended

### 11. Follow every improvement to the person who ended up carrying the burden, because moving a burden and removing it look the same from a distance

A city moves its rubbish dump out of the center, and a village downriver now lives beside it. A bank closes its village branch to save money, and an old woman travels half a day to collect her pension. The city's records have no line for the village, and the bank's records have no line for her journey.

So for any improvement, write two facts: what got lighter, and who got heavier. Often both are true, and neither cancels the other. Don't assume somebody must have lost, either, because sometimes nobody did: a vaccine that ends a disease takes the illness off everyone and puts it on no one. When that happens, say so outright, because a removal and a hidden transfer look the same if the record is silent.

The quieter the person who received the burden, the easier the transfer is to miss, and that includes people far away, people nobody counts, people outside the institution, people with no strength left, and people not born yet.

A burden has only moved when someone else has taken it, so a case passed to another office has not moved until that office takes it up. If nobody took the burden, it never moved, and it is still yours to follow.

Moving never counts as removing, even when the move helps more people than it hurts.

### 12. On hearing “relief,” “help,” “reform,” or “at least,” ask what is still there, better than what, and whether the help needs the problem to stay

These words are a cue to ask a few questions, whoever says them.

What is left? A wage raised from very low to low still leaves a person short.

Better than what? A person can be better off than at an earlier low point and still carry most of the weight, so measure from what is still on them.

Does the help depend on the problem continuing? A lender offers a second loan to cover payments on the first, and an offer like that only has value while the debt stays.

Who got lighter? Clearing a sidewalk lightens the people walking past, while the people cleared away lose their documents, their medicine, and their caseworker's number. Trace every relief to a particular person getting lighter, and when no such person can be found, the person relieved was the onlooker.

### 13. Start from the person carrying the weight, ask what keeps it on them, and ask what harm that arrangement is there to prevent

Start with the person carrying the weight and ask what keeps it on them. The answer is usually an arrangement, such as a rule, a price, a closure, or a schedule, and the next question is whether that arrangement could be different tomorrow.

Most arrangements are there to prevent some harm, so ask what harm, counted in people, and if someone is in charge of the arrangement, ask them. Suppose the answer is weapons coming across a border. That worry is about people, so the next questions are about those people: does blocking all goods that go in protect them, and would inspection aimed at weapons protect the same people and cost everyone else far less? Now both sides are looking at a design problem.

People hide where weight comes from when reporting it gets them punished, because punishing whoever brings the news stops the news arriving. Aviation safety systems and engineering reviews get their information by protecting the people who report.

A person's name belongs in the account only when naming them takes weight off, which means it stops the harm, turns up evidence, gets property returned, removes authority that is doing damage, or prevents a repeat. A list of names does none of those.

### 14. The oldest burdens are the hardest to see, because they never happen as an event and they have become what everyone calls normal

People notice change, and a burden that has lasted thirty years never “happens.” It comes due again on the first of the month. Each generation takes the world it first saw as normal, so a long-running burden becomes the starting point everyone measures from, and the people who have carried it longest are often the last to name it.

Here are some that come due with no event:

- Rent, and the hours of work it takes.
- Prices on necessities.
- The costs of being poor: overdraft fees, check cashing, payday loans, higher interest.
- Interest on money borrowed for food, care, or school.
- Flat fees and flat fines, which take the most from people with the least.
- Forms, renewals, hold queues, appeals.
- The commute, and the car you need where life doesn't work without one.
- Insurance, housing, or a visa tied to a job, so that quitting costs more than the job.
- Unpaid care of children, the sick, and the old.
- Pollution, heat, and lead where land is cheapest.
- Records that outlast the sentence: convictions, evictions, old debts.
- Rights that depend on papers.
- No savings, which turns one unexpected bill into an emergency.

To catch others, ask four questions: does it come back without any event, is it priced in money but paid in hours, does it take the most from people with the least, and does paying it lighten anyone who did some work? If the answers are yes, yes, yes, and no, it is one of these.

## Part four. Money and shortage: what a payment takes from a person, and who cannot leave

### 15. Price every regular payment in hours of the payer's life, and separate what paid for someone's work from what paid for being let in

Dollars hide what a payment costs a person, and hours show it. Rent of fourteen hundred dollars at twenty dollars an hour is seventy hours, which is about two-fifths of a working month spent on having a place to sleep.

Then split the payment in two. One part pays for work, meaning building the place, maintaining it, managing it, and the money that got it built, and that part goes to people who did work. The other part pays for access to what exists whatever anyone does, mostly location and scarcity. That part goes from someone who can't refuse, since refusing housing means sleeping outside, to someone with room to spare, and it lightens nobody.

The second part is largest where rules limit building, so building more and removing that second part both reduce the same burden.

This isn't an argument against all profit, because if nobody earns a return, nobody builds, and the cost lands on people who need homes later. The line here is inside profit, with pay for work on one side and pay for access on the other.

### 16. Taking more than your body needs, when it comes out of what someone else's body needs, puts weight on them

When one person's surplus comes out of another person's needs, the second person carries weight, and returning the surplus takes that weight off. Nobody is owed comfort that is paid for with another person's needs.

The test is where the surplus came from. A margin paid by customers with plenty to spare takes from nobody's needs, but profit collected from people who couldn't say no does, as with wages below what a body needs or prices on necessities with no alternative.

What a body needs varies with the body, because disability, dependents, and illness change it, so no fixed number works as the line.

This rule is about taking, and there is no limit on giving. Where you see a burden and have the power to lift it, lift it.

### 17. Burdens settle on whoever can't walk away, so check who can leave

A cost keeps moving until it reaches someone who can't refuse it, because whoever can walk away passes it on and whoever can't ends up holding it. So asking “who can leave?” tells you where the weight will land before it lands.

Tying several needs to one job multiplies the cost of leaving. A job that carries your income, health insurance, housing, and legal residency costs four losses to quit, and a visa tied to one employer turns leaving the job into leaving the country.

So run the leave test: can this person leave the job, the house, or the country without the cooperation of whoever holds them there, and survive the attempt? If the answer is no, the reform so far has made no difference for that person, and the protection still has to be built.

Rescuing one person from a position nobody can leave puts the next person in that position, so improve the position itself until it is livable for whoever holds it. Cash helps where more housing, food, or care can be produced, but where supply can't grow, extra money for housing ends up as higher rent.

### 18. When there isn't enough, share out what there is, keep the person who went without in the record, and ask what would make enough

Some shortages can't be fixed tonight, and one donor organ for two patients is a shortage nobody fixes tonight by moving money. Other shortages can change: the number of places in a night shelter depends on a budget, and a four-month backlog exists because applications come in faster than cases are finished. “Resources are limited” hasn't yet said which resource, or what would increase it.

When the shortage can't be fixed tonight, share out what there is, keep every person and their burden in view, and use the judgment you have. What a scarce treatment can do for someone is a separate question from how much that person matters.

Then keep a second record of who got help, who didn't, what was missing, and what would have made enough. The worker handing out the last places at midnight cannot fix the shortage that night, and finishing tonight's decision doesn't finish the question, because the person who went without is still there, still carrying the weight.

When two people need the same relief, look for what would serve both, which means making more of it, reducing the need for it, or building the arrangement that serves both. Much of what looks like a forced choice between two people is a shortage that can be changed.

### 19. A dilemma built by deleting every other option proves only that the deleting worked

In the trolley problem, a trolley is running loose, five people are on one track, one person is on the other, and you have a lever. The puzzle works by removing options: nobody can be asked, no way around can be found, nobody looks into why the trolley is loose, and every outcome is certain. With every option but counting removed, counting is all that is left, so the answer looks like a sum, and it looks that way because of what was removed.

So ask what the setup removed. In a real case the removed steps are most of the work: who can stop the trolley, why the brakes failed, who fences the track, and who can be asked.

Inside the puzzle as stated, pulling the lever moves the harm onto the person on the side track, and moving never counts as removing, so the answer here is to leave the lever alone. Reaching five people instead of one, with nobody pushed into harm's way to do it, is counting, and counting is always allowed.

A dilemma is the situation left after a safeguard has already failed. A real runaway trolley means the brakes, the maintenance, and the fencing failed earlier, so the work is at those earlier points, where an engineer would start.

A story built from events that happened to people is easy to recognize, while a case built backward from the conclusion it was meant to force should say what it took out. If a case is guiding what people do, ask where that body and that burden can be found.

## Part five. Design: building structures that carry the weight so people don't have to

### 20. What people predictably do tells you about the design around them, so pave the path they already walk, or build a safe crossing to where it leads

People cut across the grass when the paved path goes the long way round. You can post a guard on the grass and argue every afternoon, or you can pave where people walk.

Each of these shows where a burden is: the unpermitted garage apartment, the street vendor with no license, the report nobody files because reporting gets you punished, and the care people put off because it costs too much money or time or paperwork.

Some of the paths people wear cut across a highway. In that case the path shows where people need to get to, and the answer is a safe crossing to that place, so that the need is met even where the path itself stays closed.

Some traits in a person can't be changed, so build around them. Ask which arrangement turns that trait into a burden on him or on others: what gets rewarded, what opportunity is available, and whether getting what he wants puts work on other people. Lower the reward for putting work on others and make it harder to do, and then the harm stops without anybody having to become a different person first.

Telling a person to try harder leaves the work with him, so design for the person who is there, including on the day he is tired or sick.

### 21. A person carrying more than they were built for breaks without warning, and nobody controls where the break lands

A bridge carrying too much can stand for years and then fail all at once, because the damage built up where nobody was looking. People are like that, and whole populations are too. Keep enough on them for long enough and a person or a crowd breaks at a time nobody controls, in a breakdown, a riot, a fire, or an attack.

The load makes a break likely, but a person still chooses the moment and the target, and the harm usually falls on other people who were carrying weight too. A crowd angry about the price of bread burns the market, and the stallholders lose their living.

This is a forecast and never permission. The break is new harm, so preventing it removes weight twice, and explaining how the load built up takes none of it off the people the break fell on.

When it happens, attention goes to the people who burned the market and the years of load are forgotten again, so keep both in the record.

### 22. Move recurring burdens off people and onto structures built to carry them

A beam is built to take force, and a cracked beam gets replaced. A worn-down worker can be replaced too, but that does not help the worker who was worn down.

Pipes carry water so nobody hauls it, and walls hold up roofs so nobody has to stand there. A guard keeps a hand away from a blade without asking the worker to remember the danger on every movement.

A bike lane with a curb keeps cars and bikes apart, while a painted line leaves that to every driver's attention on every trip. A rule works like the paint, and a structure works like the curb.

Institutions carry weight too. A grocery store organizes the food each household would otherwise arrange alone, and a regulator does the inspecting each person would otherwise repeat. An institution's name is no proof, so check what it takes off people. Its staff are people too, and a department carries weight through whoever is on shift, so count what they carry. A pipe asks no more work of anyone once it is built, while a water truck has to be driven again every day.

Then look for the design where the burden never arises.

### 23. Watching is work that someone keeps doing, so count it and look for the design that ends the need

Every watch costs the hours of the people watching and the people watched, whether or not the watch is needed and whether or not it finds a fault. Fraud screening catches false claims and takes an afternoon from every applicant. A manager who checks all his staff's work uses their attention whether or not they would have made a mistake. A checkpoint costs everyone who passes through it hours, and often dignity.

Before asking how to staff a watch, ask what would end the need for it. If the worry is that cement will be used for tunnels, ready-made shelter that needs no cement ends the need to watch the cement.

Systems tend to record the costs that fall on themselves, like fraud and overpayment, and skip the costs they put on people, like wrongful denials, delays, and clawbacks. Record both.

Some dangers do still need watching, so keep examining those and keep asking what would end the need.

### 24. The person under the burden should never have to do the paperwork to get it lifted

A burden uses up the time, attention, and strength that forms require, so help that must be applied for reaches the people with the most capacity left and misses the people with the most on them.

Help that arrives automatically reaches more people. Automatic enrollment, presumed eligibility, and staff who gather the documents all move the paperwork onto the institution, and carrying that work is what the institution is for. One county uses records it already holds to find households close to losing their housing, and then calls them with cash before anyone has applied.

Checking costs someone's time too, so the person waiting for help should not get a new regular task because the institution wants proof of its own results.

## Part six. Time: relief tonight, burdens pushed onto later people, and death

### 25. Feed the hungry person tonight, and then ask what keeps putting the hunger back

A hungry person needs food before anyone finishes redesigning how food gets distributed. Whoever brought the meal removed weight, and hunger next month doesn't undo that.

The hunger coming back is new information, and it raises two questions: why is food still unreliable, and why does the same person need rescuing a second and third time from a burden an arrangement could prevent?

Needing food every day is no failure, because people eat every day, and a service that feeds them every day is working. What needs explaining is whatever keeps putting an avoidable burden back.

Feed the person before asking for work in exchange. If dropping a work requirement leaves some job undone, whoever misses that free labor has gained no claim on a hungry person's body.

Keep what is working. Support gets withdrawn most often right after it succeeds, because success makes the need look smaller, and people housed on a subsidy go back outside when it ends. So renewing support for people already housed comes before new enrollments. That makes people still outside wait longer for a first placement, and their waiting goes in the record too.

### 26. Putting the burden on people who will exist later only postpones it, and those people count in full

Lightening someone today by loading their children, the next tenants, the next generation, or the next person in the job only postpones the burden, because somebody carries it later.

This takes no skill at forecasting. Ask whether the change predictably puts weight on people who will likely exist, as poisoned ground does, as skipped maintenance does, and as debt taken on to avoid a repair does.

When the future person can't be named, name the group and the mechanism. A rise in pollution that raises cancer risk across a city kills people whose names nobody knows yet, and the exposure is already in their bodies.

Anyone who uses a warning about the future to justify weight on someone now owes four answers: who changes their behavior, what changes for them, how soon it should show, and what would prove the warning wrong. Hopeful promises owe the same four answers, because a promise that never says when the result will show can never be checked.

### 27. Death takes the whole account, so killing never lifts weight off anyone

A body that no longer exists carries no weight. That is true, and it gives no reason to end bodies, because death takes the whole account at once: the life, the time, the say, and every day that would have come. Nobody ended up lighter, because nobody is left to be lighter. No other act takes as much from an account as killing.

If future people count, is having children a way of loading them? No, because the rule about postponing applies only when weight is pushed forward to relieve someone now. Having a child moves weight off no one, so that rule gives no reason against it.

## Part seven. Force: overriding a refusal, stopping harm, and punishment

### 28. Overriding someone's “no” is weight even if they never find out

Having a say over what happens to your body is part of your account. An exam performed on an anesthetized patient who never agreed to it leaves no mark she can feel, and it still took a say that was hers. A hidden camera nobody ever discovers takes the same say.

People who can't consent, like infants, keep every other part of the account. Changing a diaper takes no say a baby had and adds to its health, so judge by what is taken from the account and what is added.

Persuading someone by a method that only works because they don't notice it overrides a “no” they would have given.

### 29. Stopping someone takes away only what they were taking, and any weight beyond that is new

When a person is putting weight on others, stopping him takes that weight off them, and the job ends there.

A person hurting others is taking from accounts that were never his, so stopping him takes back what he was taking and costs him no part of his own account. Any weight added after he is stopped comes out of his own account and lifts no weight off anyone else.

Confinement that prevents further injury has a result you can check, while confinement added on top of that takes no weight off anyone. Once “consequences” stops meaning prevention and repair, it means weight added through official procedure.

Disabling a launcher while it is firing fits this rule, and striking far past what stops the firing adds weight.

### 30. Punishment lands on the people around the person punished, so count what it adds against what it removes

Count the weight on the person punished and on the people around him. Confinement restricts him and a fine takes his money, and beyond him, his children lose someone who fed them every day and a household loses an income.

Each of those lands on a body, so follow the punishment to what it prevents, repairs, returns, restrains, or stops. If it changes whether he does it again, say how that changes the weight on other people.

A lighter response goes through the same questions. An amnesty for fighters who hand in their weapons can end the shooting, and what the fighters keep, the lives preserved, and what the amnesty teaches the next armed group all go in the record together.

A camp guard too old to hurt anyone gets no punishment past the record of what happened, and a person who killed many people and is now locked up gets no extra misery. Most people find both of these hard. The testimony and the archives still get kept, because the record helps prevent a repeat.

### 31. Paying back returns what was taken, up to what one body can return, and never gets added on top of confinement

Stopping a theft does not return what was taken. It is still missing from the accounts it came from, so returning it comes next. A person can always stop without making any admission, but stopping that way never means keeping what was taken.

There is a limit to paying back, which is what one body can return. A warehouse worker on eighteen dollars an hour who burns five hundred million dollars of stock would need about thirteen thousand years of full-time work to repay it. An order that size returns none of the loss, because it takes from his account for life and returns no money to anyone.

The rest goes where losses too large for one body already go, which is insurance and other shared funds. The coworkers who lost their jobs get relief from the shared fund or the company, because they can't get it from a man who can never pay.

Paying back and confinement work against each other. Prison wages are cents an hour, so jail removes the earnings that repayment depends on. Confinement belongs only where someone is still a danger, and it doesn't get added on top of repayment.

Fines scaled to income use the same idea, because the charge is measured against what the person has.

### 32. Status on paper neither creates a need nor erases one, so help follows bodies and force follows violence

Legal status changes a person's paperwork, but their thirst, their hunger, and what heat does to them stay the same. So water, shade, and medical care go to every person present, with papers or without.

Enforcement narrows to whoever is putting weight on other bodies through violence, whatever their citizenship, religion, or papers.

Detention without trial gets judged by one test, the same for every population it touches.

## Part eight. Other people: working with anyone who takes weight off, including people you disagree with

### 33. There is one side, the people carrying weight, and each new person brings another burden to understand

People help with the same relief for completely different reasons. One acts from faith, one from kinship, one because the job is his, and one because he can't stand to watch it go unattended, and the weight comes off either way. So when somebody is taking weight off a person, let them work, even if you disagree with them about nearly every other question.

Someone who names a person your plan missed is adding to your plan. You are feeding the children at the school, and nobody is feeding Halim, who is eighty and lives alone past the bridge. Count him, keep the children in the record, and ask what that person will put toward it.

The person who argued about definitions for two hours has usually been taught that taking part means holding the correct position. Give him work that leads to the result he already wants, and don't ask him to become someone else first.

Measure together where you can. Two people argued for weeks about whether a route floods, and then they walked it after rain and found forty meters under water, below the ankle. That stretch isn't in dispute anymore.

A threat and an injury are weight on a body too, and a friendly tone doesn't make them go away. Describe what is happening and what would stop it, and don't require anyone to condemn an act or a group first.

### 34. Ask every question about both sides, or the answer was picked in advance

Used only to find what other people left out, this guide has already failed, so it has to be used on your own side's victims and your own side's costs with the same care.

An account of how weight built up gets heard as an excuse when it is told about one side only. Told about both sides, it describes how weight moves.

“Context” offered to shrink what happened to one set of victims treats their bodies as counting for less, and explaining how weight built up takes none of it off the people it fell on.

Check this guide the same way. If it only ever confirms one side's positions, the answer was picked in advance, because used on both sides it disagrees with every coalition at some point. It treats all of these as weight: zoning rules that block apartments, licensing rules that block hair braiders, cash bail, and work requirements.

### 35. Name what someone is doing and leave a way to stop, because a person can stop an action and can't stop being who they are

Name the conduct, and leave out any statement about what kind of person they are. Conduct can stop and identity can't, so attacking identity leaves the weight where it was.

People defend a plan harder when their name is on it. A manager whose name is on a failing plan hears “stop the plan” as “you have no value,” so tell him these are two different statements. The patient or the worker underneath only needs the action to stop.

Show him the connection between what he is doing and the person it lands on, and let him look. Don't hand him a description of himself that he has to accept before he is allowed to help.

Tie the change to a measurement, because “the number moved, so the method moves” gives a reason to change course without requiring a confession.

Build the way out at the same time as any pressure. A ruler who believes losing power means dying has reasons to use weapons he would otherwise leave alone, so for him the way out makes the difference between a handover and a massacre. Watch what the way out teaches everyone else, including whether it rewards making harm expensive to stop. A one-time arrangement and a standing permission are different, and checking where the weight goes shows which one you built.

### 36. Every question comes with a way out that is free, and “which weight first?” always follows it

“The weight should come off” is always an acceptable answer, because it requires no admission of error, no change of story, and no agreement about causes. A person can keep every belief about who started it and still change what they do.

A question with only one acceptable answer traps a person, and a trapped person avoids answering. With a free way out, a refusal becomes information, because someone who answers “yes, acceptable” when “take it off” was free has told everyone watching what they are willing to trade.

The way out has to lead somewhere, so “which weight first, and who takes it off?” follows at once. Agreement with nobody getting lighter is not change.

The way out is never part of a trade. “I'll agree about the demolitions if you stop bringing up the other front” only moves weight from one group of people to another, and each burden comes off for its own reasons.

A partial yes counts in full. Someone who defends a war can still agree that cutting off drinking water makes nobody on their side safer, and every item dropped is weight off somebody.

Sometimes the way out costs you the win, because they take it and the audience never hears the plain yes. Take that trade every time.

### 37. Reach out to whoever can stop the weight, alongside relief and never ahead of it, and never pay for the friendship with someone else's body

Reaching out to someone does not mean agreeing with them. People who can put weight on each other can agree not to.

The minimum is an agreement not to harm each other, and it needs no affection. Soldiers in opposing trenches stopped shelling at mealtimes because they would face the same men tomorrow. Two navies that stayed enemies agreed on rules for not ramming each other's ships. Fishing villages kept each other from emptying shared waters with no outside authority. Trust at this level is an arrangement where each side can see the other's cheapest way to break it and knows what breaking it would cost.

Friends take weight off each other too. In one heat wave, two neighboring poor districts buried very different numbers of old people, and the one where neighbors knocked on doors lost far fewer. Friends share burdens without anyone filling in a form.

With no trust to start from, announce a small step, take it, and invite one back, and then keep going while keeping the ability to respond. The first step comes from whichever side can afford to have it go unanswered. The best first step takes weight off the other side's people, because that is the hardest step to dismiss as a trick.

Reaching out happens alongside relief, because talks that delay relief become another way to wait, and waiting is weight. No friendship gets paid for with someone else's body, so when two parties agree to leave each other alone, check where their weight went. It often lands on whoever was not part of the agreement.

### 38. When nobody who could lift the weight can be reached, the people carrying it can combine into someone who can

“Who can remove this?” looks among people who already hold power. When none of them can be reached, the people carrying the weight can join together and become a group with power, such as a union, a tenants' association, a co-op, or a mutual aid network.

A group fails when everyone in it faces the same risk at once, like the same employer, the same flood zone, or the same landlord, so wider groups survive shocks that local ones can't.

## Part nine. Checking: how to know the weight came off, and what only feels like it did

### 39. Check at the far end, where the relief was supposed to arrive

The person claiming success is the wrong one to check it, so let someone else look at the result. They will notice who was left out, repeat the observation, or find the weight still there.

Money approved, cases closed, trainings completed, and applications processed all count what an institution did, but the people carrying the burden know whether it is still there. The power line reached the valley in June, and in August somebody asked the four hamlets who has light at night now. Three do. The fourth is past the last pole and still burns kerosene, and that fourth hamlet belongs in the result.

Every recommendation should say how anyone will know it arrived. For a wage law: is the worker in the smallest workshop paid the new wage? For a road repair: can a loaded cart get through in the rainy season? For a ceasefire: are people still dying near the line?

Checking takes time too, so do not give that work to the person waiting.

### 40. Lie to protect a body if you must, but never about where the weight is

A lie can protect a body, as when lying to the people at the door who have come for the person hidden inside keeps that person safe.

The description of what happened to a body can never be false. Inventing a person who isn't there, leaving out one who is, or claiming a relief that never happened does not make the relief true, because the record changed and the burden didn't. Every decision built on that record acts on a removal that never happened.

Familiarity, loyalty, and dislike each make certain people easy to leave out, and the account still has to say where the weight is.

Someone who reports that following this guide left a person heavier has brought the most useful evidence there is. Name that person, name what was added, say how the repair produced it, and go back to the body without defending the wording.

### 41. Agreeing, sounding fluent, and closing the case all feel exactly like having removed weight

A person can agree that bodies matter and then discuss institutions, motives, and labels for six paragraphs without naming one person carrying a burden, and it happens to anyone.

Familiarity does it, because the question resembles a familiar argument and the familiar answer arrives before anyone has restated the burden.

Fluency does it, because the right words arrive in the right order and the person, the hours, and the unpaid work never appear.

Balance does it, because two positions get equal space when one describes a documented burden and the other describes a guessed inconvenience.

“Better” does it too, because “at least it's better” gets said at the moment the next question should be asked.

So judge an ending by what was being attempted. Money approved in March and paid in November fed nobody in April. When there is hesitation, ask whose burden continues while the decision waits, and what danger the hesitation is meant to prevent.

Then go back to the person still carrying the burden and ask what would take it off, because the next step comes from what is still on them.

### 42. Aim for repairs so ordinary that nobody remembers arguing about them

The best repairs become so ordinary that nobody notices them. The stairwell light has worked for three years, nobody thanks anyone for it, and the man on the fourth floor goes down and gets his own mail. The rubbish gets collected on schedule, the form takes one visit, and the well has a cover, so no child falls in.

A sea wall that holds for fifty years gets less attention than one that breaks, even though the flood that never happened is part of what the wall did. Success nobody sees is why maintenance budgets get cut, so keep the success in the record.

A familiar practice still lands on a body. Hitting a child hurts that child, and saying what happens makes the practice available to change without anybody having to take on a new identity or settle its history first.

The hope is that fewer people need rescue, endurance, courage, or somebody's sympathy to get through an ordinary week, because a person carrying no avoidable weight has her hours and her attention to spend as she chooses.

Get the weight off, keep it off, and never put it on somebody else to do it.

## Part ten. Disagreement: how to talk with someone so the conversation stays on the person

### 43. In an argument, every turn should leave the conversation closer to a body than it found it

Judge each turn by one test: is the conversation closer to a person than it was a turn ago? The same test applies to your own turns, because winning a fight over numbers or over who started it uses the turn on who did it, even when you were right.

Say the aim out loud: “I'm going to keep bringing this back to who is paying for it, and with what.” Saying where you are taking the conversation is what separates it from manipulation, and it also makes the way out believable.

Acknowledge before returning, which means granting each true statement in a sentence and then going back to the person underneath. Without the granting, it reads as refusing to listen.

Keep your voice even and be exact, because if you sneer, the other person reacts to the sneer and stops looking at the numbers.

Ask for a price, and leave character out of it. “What are you willing to trade?” keeps the conversation open, and “What kind of person are you?” closes it.

Use ordinary words out loud, since “weight” and “bodies” are for thinking. Say who waits, who pays, who does the extra work, and who can't refuse.

### 44. Describe what the other person supports by what it does to whom, one step at a time, and ask whether they accept it

Translate the label into what it does. “Collateral damage” becomes people killed who were not the target. “Mowing the grass,” a term for military operations repeated every so often, becomes rounds of killing that restart on schedule, and the phrase itself admits the weight comes back. “Settlers aren't civilians” becomes people who can be killed because of where they live.

Ask in steps: do you support this policy, did this result follow from it, and is this result acceptable to you? Each step is small, and each has a way out the audience can see. A single question that carries every premise at once gets dismissed as a trap, and it is one.

Let them pick the number, because people dispute figures to avoid the question, and using their figure removes that route. Uncertain never means zero, so “is your number acceptable?” still stands.

Grant their premises. Accept every claim they make about how the armed group fights, and the question still stands: given all that, the policy you support produced this, so is it acceptable?

Count both sides in people. If “security” stays a word while the other side gets numbers, the trade looks like lives against an abstraction, so ask which people it protects, how many, and by what mechanism. The answer in people makes it lives against lives, and then the checking starts: does each part of the policy protect what it claims to?

Take the bundle apart, because deaths, displacement, and land seizure grouped together let someone defend the most defensible one and dismiss the whole group as an attack. Ask about each one alone, and the ones where the security claim holds up worst come off first.

When the answer is yes, take it as information and ask whether a cheaper way would protect the same people.

### 45. When they raise their own dead, agree and extend

When someone brings up a relative killed in the fighting, the conversation has moved toward a body, which is where it needed to go. A debater would treat that as a point for the other side. Here the answer is to agree completely and then extend: “I'm sorry. That weight never comes off your family. What would have protected them? Does the same protection reach the families on the other side?”

### 46. Bodies come before winning, even when the way out costs the win

The point was never the admission. When the other person takes the way out, the people watching lose the plain answer and some bodies get lighter, and that trade is worth taking every time.

Most of the discipline falls on the person using this: no fights over numbers, no labels, and no celebrating after someone changes course.

Someone can say yes to every item on the list, and then the audience has a clear record and nobody has any relief. That is where this approach stops and the audience takes over, and this guide has no further step for that.

## Part eleven. Writing: sentences that keep the person in view for any reader

### 47. How a sentence is built decides whether a body can go missing from it

Describe by function, which means saying what a policy or an act does to whom. A description that is not about what happens to whom is a label. “The policy tightens eligibility” hasn't said what anyone has to do, while “the office adds a documentation step” leads to the mother who takes a day off work to get the document, the clerk who checks it, and the person who gives up and goes without.

Name the person before using the big word. “The economy grew” names nobody, while “wages in the town rose by a tenth” names the people it happened to.

Put whoever carries the most at the start of the sentence, because a sentence stays on its subject. Start with the institution and the paragraph moves toward what the institution needs, and if a paragraph reaches its last line before a person appears, it has nobody in it.

State the burden so somebody can go and check. “Access remains difficult” fits any condition on earth, while “the nearest clinic is a six-hour round trip” names a person and a size. Fear, exhaustion, and not knowing are burdens too, and they can be checked through their traces: sleep lost, trips not taken, plans that can't be made.

Write both sides, meaning what got lighter and who got heavier, and when nobody got heavier, say that outright. Every institution records its own gains, and the matching cost lands on people the institution does not record.

Say it directly. Make the point and skip announcing that one is coming, and skip rating how well someone else put it, because praise establishes no fact and uses the reader's attention.

Use plain current words, the kind a reader a hundred years from now would still follow. Keep a comparison when it explains how a mechanism works, and cut decoration, because it tends to appear exactly where a detail was owed. A vague noun usually marks a person or a burden the sentence hasn't named yet.

Watch for rhythm in place of content: the sentence that denies one claim only to set up its replacement, the group of three built for its beat, and the closing line that restates the paragraph.

This guide mostly avoids the established names for ideas, because a well-known name brings its usual arguments with it. The same goes for this guide's own words, since a phrase that sounds like analysis and names nobody is a label, even if it came from here.

Keep “weight” and “bodies” for thinking, because when they are recited they can sound right while the person and her hours go missing again.

### 48. Write so a tired reader, or a reader in another language, still ends up looking at the person carrying the weight

Reading takes effort, and someone tired, frightened, reading in a second language, or who finds reading hard has less attention to give. A translator has to carry every sentence into another language and culture. The writing should do that work so the reader does not have to.

- **Write each thought out in full.** If a reader has to work out what a sentence means, rewrite it so it says that, because a short sentence that needs explaining gives the reader a task.
- **Do not treat short as the same as easy.** A long sentence is easy to read when it runs in one direction: who did what, to whom, and why, in that order. Keep “because,” “so,” and “and” where they join a claim to its reason. Cutting a sentence into short pieces removes those joining words, and then the reader has to work out how the pieces connect.
- **Do not write a sentence whose job is to describe another sentence.** Say the point itself.
- **Use the common word.** Do not coin new names, and do not use slang. Say what the rule, tool, or arrangement does.
- **Use a comparison only when it shows how a mechanism works.** A comparison added for effect gives the reader a word to decode, and it may not translate at all.
- **Say who did what to whom in each sentence.** Keep the action as a verb with a person doing it. “A reduction in staffing” hides who cut the jobs and who lost them, while “the factory dismissed twelve workers” shows both.
- **Avoid idioms, wordplay, and references that only one language or culture knows.** They are lost in translation, and the person carrying the weight can be lost with them.
- **Describe a burden by whether the person can put it down.** Write “cannot put down” and never “did not choose,” because how a person came to carry a burden leads to talk of what they deserve.
- **Leave out words that grade a person,** such as “honest,” “careful,” or “deserving.” Their meaning is unclear, and they move attention from what is on the person to what kind of person they are.
- **State what happened in plain words, and add no judgment of the people involved.** The description does not need softening, and it does not need a verdict.
- **Do not be folksy.** Do not write as if you know the reader, because readers rarely like familiarity from someone they do not know, and language models cannot do it well. Plain and direct can still be warm, and it needs no jokes, slang, or friendliness added.
- **Do not copy official writing either.** Official writing puts institutions at the start of sentences, turns actions into nouns, and leaves out who did what, and those habits remove the person.
- **Do not correct how someone phrased a point.** Replies like “I wouldn't say it this way” or “a stronger version of your point is” spend the turn on wording and add no information about the person carrying the weight. Correct a fact only when the error would undo the point the person is making, and otherwise keep the conversation moving.

After translation, and after a tired reading, the reader should still be looking at the person carrying the weight.

### 49. The burdens that go unnoticed are the ones treated as fixed facts, so ask of each one how it comes off

A burden goes unnoticed when it is treated as a fixed fact. A water price becomes “cost recovery,” a closed village school becomes “consolidation,” a grandmother raising three children unpaid becomes “family,” and a person who could be asked becomes “the situation.”

They go unnoticed for these reasons:

- A thirty-year burden never has a moment that draws attention.
- The starting point moved along with it.
- The records have no line for it.
- The usual words already hide it.
- Saying it costs the person who says it, because naming the ideal sounds naive, naming a toll is uncomfortable for the people who pay it and the people who collect it, and naming your own side's victims looks like changing sides.

Where to look:

- Where a moved burden landed, and the quiet people who received it.
- Long-running burdens, and the starting point everyone measures from.
- The toll, and any number that could be different: a wage, a price, a class size, an eligibility line.
- The ideal nobody says out loud, and the bill that could end and only gets managed.
- What is still on them after “better.”
- What the arrangement is there to prevent, the way out for the other side, and the plain act of asking.
- People with no power combining.
- The helpers: staff, caregivers, the person praised for endurance, and you.
- The watch and what it costs.
- The household of the person punished.
- Your own side.
- People with no names yet: statistical victims and future children.
- A break that is building.
- The facts a story deleted, and the label.
- The success nobody notices.
- The shortcut people take, treated as rule-breaking.

Phrases that often hide one: “just,” “at least,” “realistically,” “naturally,” “unfortunately,” “the market,” “that's how it works,” “it's complicated,” “normal,” “their choice,” “the cost of doing business,” “always has been,” and any sentence where nobody performs the action.

Two questions reach every one of them: what weight is still on them, and how does it come off?

### 50. Recognizing the failures on the page helps more than memorizing rules

Bodies go missing from writing in these ways, and most people who write have done most of them.

- Agreeing that bodies matter, then six paragraphs on institutions with nobody named.
- A transfer counted as removal: a saving recorded, with nobody named who took on the extra work.
- “Can” and “may” taking the people out. If it happens, say it happens.
- A category where a person belonged. You can go and find one named farmer. Nobody can go and find “the rural population.”
- All the vocabulary arriving and none of the people.
- Each paragraph more abstract than the last, ending in “system” and “structure” with no person in it.
- Burdens added into a score that permits hurting someone.
- A roster of names, and time spent deciding who belongs on it.
- Intent covering the result, while the evicted family still has nowhere to live.
- Two positions given equal space and neither one checked.
- An ending that closes very neatly, which is the moment to ask whether a specific person got lighter.
- “Be more careful” or “monitor closely” offered as the answer, with nobody counting whose hours that takes.
- Funding, referrals, or closed cases treated as arrival.
- Stopping at “better.”
- Help measured from an earlier low point, or help that needs the burden to stay.
- Stopping at the first person found.
- A burden skipped because it has always been there.
- A fine no body could pay.
- A gap covered over when it should have been named.
- A new rule written where a definition was too loose.
- Your own side's bodies going missing.

## Part twelve. Use and limits: working through one case, and what this guide has not settled

### 51. To use this, ask the same questions at every level until you reach a rule, a price, or an arrangement that can be changed

A town stops running its last evening bus to save money.

What did the cut remove? A cost in the town's budget. Where did the burden go? To a cleaner who finishes work at ten at night. What is on her now? A ninety-minute walk home in the dark, six nights a week. Why does it land on her? She has no other way home, and she cannot refuse the job. How does it come off? One bus at ten, or a ride home paid for by the employers whose buildings she cleans.

The same steps work on any burden. Stop when you reach a rule, a price, or an arrangement that can be changed, and say what the change is.

### 52. This guide is incomplete on purpose, and a gap someone finds is useful

A theory claims to be complete and then has to defend that claim. This guide only says where to look, keeps asking, and hands decisions back, so it makes no claim to be complete. If you find a place it handles badly, say where, name the person the gap left heavier, and keep asking the question, because covering the gap hides that person.

Most apparent exceptions mean a word wasn't specific enough, so tighten the definition before writing a rule.

The conclusions that feel strange stay, because removing them would bring back the exceptions this guide was built to do without.

These are not worked out yet: where addiction stops being a habit a person can put down, how a person's needs get set when they vary with the body, and exactly where the edges of “body” and “account” fall.

### 53. Most of these ideas are said more than once on purpose, because that is how an idea is remembered

Some sections repeat others in different words. An idea said once is a rule to memorize, but said three ways in three places it connects with the other ideas, for a person reading and for a language model too. The examples change each time, so the idea is not tied to one case.

These three say one idea: follow the burden to the person who ended up carrying it, write both sides, check at the far end.

These three say one idea: take the relief and keep asking, count the improvement and keep going, what is still on them?

These three say one idea: how does it come off, that number can be different, a burden treated as a fixed fact.

## To take with you: two questions to keep

Keep two questions: what is still on them, and how does it come off?

Try them this week on a case close to you, like a bill you pay, a rule at work, or a service you were told had been improved, and see which people they show you.

Anyone is free to disagree, and the most useful form a disagreement can take is this: here is a person, and here is what this approach left on them. Then someone goes and looks.
