# Bodies First

Ask one question before anything else: did a load actually come off a living person, and did another person have to carry it?

A burden moved, delayed, hidden in paperwork, or pushed into next year is still being carried somewhere. A repair counts when it reaches the person it was meant to reach.

Most arguments about politics, ethics, management, and public policy lose the person surprisingly early. Someone begins with a family that cannot afford food, a patient waiting six months for treatment, a civilian being bombed, a worker doing dangerous work, or a child sleeping outside. Soon the discussion is about the economy, security, responsibility, legality, efficiency, freedom, the deficit, capitalism, socialism, crime, deterrence, or which side is worse. Those subjects matter. The person who started the argument still has to eat, sleep, heal, get home safely, or live through the night.

An abstraction gives endless escape. Faced with security, the deficit, or deterrence, anyone who would rather not concede can dispute the term or move to a different one, and the move barely shows. A body gives one escape: deny that he exists, or lie about his condition. Both are catchable and both look bad in front of other people.

Other anchors expire. Tie your ethics to a culprit and you need a new anchor every time the personnel change. Tie it to a slogan and it dies when the slogan goes out of fashion. Regimes fall, coalitions dissolve, and justifications renew themselves without end. Hunger and pain and cold will be there as long as there are people.

A city clears a park and reports that an encampment is gone while every person who slept there remains homeless. A hospital shortens stays and a daughter starts doing unpaid nursing at three in the morning. An agency approves assistance in March and sends the money in November. Each institution records progress while the person at the far end gets little or none.

So follow the result all the way through. Ask who waits longer, who pays more, who does the extra work, who becomes less safe, who loses sleep, and who has no practical way to refuse. If one person becomes lighter because another person was made heavier, the original burden was reassigned. The same holds when today's relief becomes next year's debt, when a problem disappears from a report while remaining in someone's life, or when a temporary improvement ends and the same person needs rescue again.

Real gains stay visible too. Someone becomes better off even when a solution is imperfect. Keep the costs beside the gains instead of letting one erase the other. Two people's suffering does not become easier to understand after it is converted into one total.

Recurring work belongs on structures. Bodies make poor support beams. People need sleep. Their attention fails. They get frightened, distracted, sick, and old. Institutions, buildings, machines, and infrastructure exist partly because recurring work can be placed somewhere that has no nervous system.

A painted bike lane asks every driver and cyclist to stay alert on every trip. A protected lane with a curb removes much of that demand from both of them. The curb holds when the driver is tired and when the cyclist looks away for a second. Its value includes the crashes prevented. It also includes thousands of journeys where nobody spends attention wondering whether the next car will drift across the line.

Vigilance belongs in the account because vigilance is work. An honest applicant who spends an afternoon proving he is not committing fraud paid something for the fraud-control system. A worker who watches constantly for a machine's dangerous movement supplies part of that machine's safety system with his attention. A parent who calls an agency every week to confirm a benefit was not cancelled by mistake is doing administrative work for that agency.

Some monitoring is necessary. Necessity does not make it free. The next question is whether design can remove the need for some of it.

Immediate relief does not wait for a perfect system. Feed the person who is hungry tonight. If the same person was hungry last month and will be hungry next month, the recurrence is information: something upstream still leaves hunger on him. The emergency help succeeded tonight, and the repeated emergency deserves investigation.

One donated organ and two patients is a real shortage tonight. A hospital with forty beds instead of sixty has a history. An office with a four-month backlog has an arrival rate, a completion rate, staffing decisions, procedures, and budgets. A shortage is completely real in the present and still produced by earlier choices. Allocate what exists when allocation is unavoidable, then keep asking why the shortage exists and whether it has to exist again.

A person commits terrible acts. Describe those acts accurately. Preserve the evidence. Stop dangerous conduct immediately where stopping it is possible. None of that requires turning the person into a monster, a fascist, an enemy of humanity, or another identity he now has to defend.

Attacking someone's standing creates a second problem to manage. A manager who could change a failed policy has less room to change it once abandoning the policy also means publicly accepting that he is stupid or evil. A ruler who believes losing power means dying has reasons to use capabilities that would have stayed unused if leaving office were survivable. A person whose identity is under attack spends his attention defending that identity. Meanwhile the people harmed by the original conduct are still waiting.

Pressure needs an exit. A changed measurement provides one. If the waiting time fell, the method changes. If violence stopped, pressure eases on terms set in advance. Nobody needs a confession before a useful action becomes possible. Accountability still matters wherever it stops conduct, protects someone, returns what was taken, uncovers evidence, or prevents repetition.

Punishment goes through the same examination. Prison prevents someone from attacking another person. A sanction deters conduct. Seized money restores resources to people who lost them. Those effects can be checked. Punishment also creates new costs for the punished person and often for children, partners, employees, or the people around them, and those costs are real regardless of what he did. Punishment gets credit for what it prevents, repairs, restrains, or deters. Suffering added because suffering feels deserved has added suffering. Leniency gets no special protection either. Mercy that leaves preventable victims to be hurt has consequences. Every idea goes through the same question.

No ideological label fits this. Public provision gets no automatic approval. Markets get no automatic rejection. Regulation, abolition, punishment, forgiveness, nationalization, privatization, deterrence, sanctions, policing, welfare, and austerity all have to reach the people they claim to help. A favored method fails sometimes. A disliked institution does useful work sometimes. Loyalty to a side cannot make a person disappear from the account.

An objection improves a proposal instead of merely blocking it. Someone who says a school meal program misses the eighty-year-old man living alone has found another person who needs including. Someone who points out that a safer process adds three hours of paperwork has found a cost. Put that person and those three hours into the problem and keep designing.

People who disagree about principles still measure the same road, count the same missed meals, record the same waiting time, or see that the same family remains without shelter. A shared measurement gives two people something to work on without either surrendering an identity first. Nobody needs sympathy, ideological agreement, or admirable motives to notice that a six-hour trip to a clinic became twenty minutes.

This asks less of the people involved. They do not have to become better people before they produce a better result. They do not have to join the correct side. They keep their history, their politics, and most of their explanation for how the problem arose. The requirement is an honest account of what is happening to the people involved and a willingness to change the arrangement when the result stays bad.

Institutions need the same honesty. Applications processed, beds funded, arrests made, grants approved, trainings completed, ceasefires signed, and dollars appropriated all describe institutional activity. They are often necessary steps. The check belongs farther away, where the intended result should appear. Did the patient get treated? Did the family move indoors? Did the worker stop getting injured? Did the money arrive before the rent was due? Did the killing stop after the ceasefire was signed?

Winning an argument changes very little by itself. Calling an opponent an accurate bad name changes nothing. Announcing a program changes nothing until the program reaches someone. Good intentions explain why a decision was made and leave its consequences exactly where they fell.

The aim is ordinary life requiring less endurance. The stairwell light works, so an older man does not need help with the stairs. Water comes through a pipe, so nobody spends four hours carrying it. A protected lane lets someone ride to work without treating every passing car as a possible emergency. A benefit arrives when it is supposed to, so a family does not spend the last week of every month wondering whether an administrative error will leave them without food.

Those results attract less attention than arguments, rescues, punishments, scandals, and declarations. The person living the result gets the time and attention back.
