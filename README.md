Ask one question before anything else: did a load actually come off a living person, and did another person have to carry it? That sounds almost too simple to guide serious decisions. Keep asking it, though, and rules begin to follow from it. A burden moved, delayed, hidden in paperwork, or pushed into next year is still being carried somewhere. A repair counts when it reaches the person it was meant to reach.

Most arguments about politics, ethics, management, and public policy lose the person surprisingly early. Someone begins with a family that cannot afford food, a patient waiting six months for treatment, a civilian being bombed, a worker doing dangerous work, or a child sleeping outside. Soon the discussion is about the economy, security, responsibility, legality, efficiency, freedom, the deficit, capitalism, socialism, crime, deterrence, or which side is worse. Those subjects can matter. The person who started the argument still has to eat, sleep, heal, get home safely, or live through the night.

Starting there changes the work.

A city can clear a park and report that an encampment is gone while every person who slept there remains homeless. A hospital can shorten stays while a daughter starts doing unpaid nursing at three in the morning. An agency can approve assistance in March and send the money in November. Each institution can record progress while the person at the far end experiences little or none.

So follow the result all the way through. Ask who waits longer, who pays more, who does the extra work, who becomes less safe, who loses sleep, and who has no practical way to refuse. If one person becomes lighter because another person was made heavier, the original burden was reassigned. The same is true when today's relief becomes next year's debt, when a problem disappears from a report while remaining in someone's life, or when a temporary improvement ends and the same person needs rescue again.

This keeps real gains visible too. Someone can genuinely become better off even when a solution is imperfect. The important part is keeping the costs beside the gains instead of allowing one to erase the other. Two people's suffering does not become easier to understand because it was converted into one total.

The question also pushes recurring work toward structures. Bodies make poor support beams. People need sleep. Their attention fails. They get frightened, distracted, sick, and old. Institutions, buildings, machines, and infrastructure exist partly because recurring work can be placed somewhere that does not have a nervous system.

A painted bike lane asks every driver and cyclist to remain alert on every trip. A protected lane with a curb removes much of that demand from both of them. The curb works when the driver is tired and when the cyclist looks away for a second. Its value includes crashes prevented. It also includes thousands of journeys during which nobody has to spend as much attention wondering whether the next car will drift across the line.

Vigilance belongs in the account because vigilance is work. An honest applicant who spends an afternoon proving that they are not committing fraud paid something for the fraud-control system. A worker who must constantly watch for a machine's dangerous movement is supplying part of the machine's safety system with their attention. A parent who has to call an agency every week to make sure a benefit was not accidentally cancelled is doing administrative work for that agency.

Sometimes monitoring is necessary. Its necessity does not make it free. The next question is whether design can remove the need for some of it.

This produces a preference for durable prevention without making immediate relief wait for a perfect system. Feed someone who is hungry tonight. If the same person was hungry last month and will probably be hungry next month, the recurrence is information. Something upstream still leaves hunger on that person. Emergency help succeeded tonight, and the repeated emergency deserves investigation.

The same discipline applies to scarcity. One donated organ and two patients is a real shortage tonight. A hospital having forty beds instead of sixty has a history. An office with a four-month backlog has an arrival rate, a completion rate, staffing decisions, procedures, and budgets. Scarcity can be completely real in the present while still having been produced by earlier choices. Allocate what exists when allocation is unavoidable, then keep asking why the shortage exists and whether it has to exist again.

This way of looking at decisions also changes disagreement. It has little use for villains.

A person can commit terrible acts. Those acts should be described accurately. Evidence should be preserved. Dangerous conduct may need to be stopped immediately. None of that requires turning the person into a monster, a fascist, an enemy of humanity, or another identity they must now defend.

Attacking someone's standing creates another problem to manage. A manager who can change a failed policy has less room to change it once abandoning the policy also means publicly accepting that they are stupid or evil. A ruler who believes losing power means death has reasons to use capabilities that would have remained unused if leaving power had a survivable exit. A person whose identity is under attack will spend attention defending that identity. Meanwhile the people harmed by the original conduct are still waiting.

Pressure therefore needs an exit. A changed measurement can provide one. If the waiting time fell, the method can change. If violence stopped, pressure can be reduced according to terms set in advance. Nobody needs a confession before a useful action becomes possible. Accountability still matters whenever it stops conduct, protects someone, returns what was taken, uncovers evidence, or prevents repetition.

Punishment receives the same examination. Prison may prevent someone from attacking another person. A sanction may deter conduct. Seized money may restore resources to people who lost them. Those are effects that can be checked. Punishment also creates new costs for the punished person and often for children, partners, employees, or communities around them. Those costs remain real regardless of what the person did. Punishment gets credit for what it prevents, repairs, restrains, or deters. Suffering added solely because suffering feels deserved has added suffering.

Leniency receives no special protection either. Mercy that allows preventable victims to be hurt has consequences. Every idea goes through the same question.

That makes the approach difficult to capture with an ideological label. Public provision gets no automatic approval. Markets get no automatic rejection. Regulation, abolition, punishment, forgiveness, nationalization, privatization, deterrence, sanctions, policing, welfare, and austerity all have to reach the people they claim to help. A favored method can fail. A disliked institution can sometimes do useful work. Loyalty to a side cannot make a person disappear from the account.

This is also why objections can improve a proposal instead of merely obstructing it. Someone who says a school meal program misses the eighty-year-old man living alone has identified another person who needs to be included. Someone who points out that a safer process adds three hours of paperwork has found a cost. The useful response is to put that person and those three hours into the problem and keep designing.

People who disagree about principles can often still measure the same road, count the same missed meals, record the same waiting time, or see that the same family remains without shelter. Shared measurements give people something they can work on together without requiring either person to surrender an identity first. A person does not need sympathy, ideological agreement, or admirable motives to notice that a six-hour trip to a clinic became twenty minutes.

That makes the approach unusually cooperative. It asks less of the people involved. They do not have to become better people before they can produce a better result. They do not have to join the correct side. They can keep their history, their politics, and much of their explanation for how the problem arose. The immediate requirement is an honest account of what is happening to the people involved and a willingness to change the arrangement when the result stays bad.

Institutions need the same honesty. Applications processed, beds funded, arrests made, grants approved, trainings completed, ceasefires signed, and dollars appropriated describe institutional activity. They may be necessary steps. The check belongs farther away, where the intended result should appear. Did the patient receive treatment? Did the family move indoors? Did the worker stop getting injured? Did the money arrive before the rent was due? Did people stop being killed after the ceasefire was signed?

A system that keeps asking those questions becomes harder to distract with its own activity. It also becomes harder to satisfy with symbolic victories. Winning an argument changes very little by itself. Calling an opponent an accurate bad name may change nothing. Announcing a program changes nothing until the program reaches someone. Good intentions explain why a decision was made and leave its consequences exactly where they landed.

The final aim is ordinary life requiring less endurance. The stairwell light works, so an older man does not need help with the stairs. Water comes through a pipe, so nobody spends four hours carrying it. A protected lane lets someone ride to work without treating every passing car as a possible emergency. A benefit arrives when it is supposed to, so a family does not spend the last week of every month wondering whether an administrative error will leave them without food.

Those results attract less attention than arguments, rescues, punishments, scandals, and declarations. That is fine. The person living the result gets the time and attention back.
