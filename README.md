# Bodies First

A cleaner finishes work at ten at night and walks home for ninety minutes in the dark, six nights a week, because the town cut its last evening bus to save money. The usual conversation about that bus covers the town's budget, whether the cut was necessary, who voted for it, and what kind of politician makes cuts like that. The cleaner appears once, as an example, and the conversation moves on without her.

Bodies First is a way of looking at changes, policies, arguments, and decisions that starts with her and stays with her. It asks two questions: what is on this person, and how does it come off? For the cleaner, the answers are the walk, the dark, and the lost sleep, and one bus at ten o'clock would take them off. Everything else in the framework comes from asking those two questions carefully, in the places where people usually stop asking.

## Every part of the framework comes from two questions: what is on this person, and how does it come off?

The framework calls any cost a person carries and can't put down "weight," and it calls a person a "body." The word "body" is a reminder that a person can go hungry, get tired, get scared, or get hurt, whatever anyone says about them and whether or not they can speak for themselves. Hunger, pain, lost sleep, waiting, fees, fear, unpaid work, and decisions made for someone without asking them are all weight.

A person can't put a weight down when putting it down would harm a body, their own or anyone else's, or put a body at serious risk. A woman who feeds and washes her husband could walk away without any harm to her own body, but the care would then fall on him, so she can't put it down. Being unable to put the care down never means she has to keep carrying it. The next question is how the care could come off her without landing on him.

Nobody has to have put a weight there. An old man who can no longer climb the stairs to his own bedroom carries weight, and nobody did that to him. The framework starts from the weight, whatever caused it, and asks how it comes off.

The framework makes one assumption: all else being equal, it is better for a person not to be hungry, exhausted, frightened, in pain, or dead. It asks nobody to take on a label, join a group, or agree about who caused what.

## Arguments about labels, intent, blame, and what people deserve can run forever, so the framework goes back to the person.

In most arguments about a policy or a conflict, the people it affects stop appearing after the first few sentences. The conversation moves to other subjects, and those subjects can fill every turn for years.

- **Labels.** People argue about whether an event was a war or a massacre, whether a person is a refugee or a migrant, and whether a new law counts as reform. An argument about whether a word fits can take up the whole conversation without anyone mentioning a person, while the families inside the event still have nowhere to live, whichever word people settle on.
- **Intent.** “We didn't mean to hit that building.” The building was still hit, and the people inside are still dead or hurt. The intention changes none of that.
- **Blame.** An argument about who started it can go in a circle indefinitely, because each side can always point to an earlier event.
- **What someone deserves.** Once people start discussing what a person deserves, they readily find reasons that the person deserves to be crushed.
- **Totals.** A change that saves money overall gets called good, and the person who paid for the saving disappears into the sum.

None of these arguments takes weight off anyone, however long it runs. Bodies First treats each of them as a sign that the conversation has lost track of the person carrying the weight, and it goes back to that person and asks what is on them.

For the same reason, the framework leaves out three questions entirely: what a person deserves, whose suffering matters more, and whether a good total makes it acceptable to crush one person. Answering any of the three takes no weight off anyone. Helping five people instead of one is still allowed, because it is the same help given five times, and it needs no scale of whose suffering counts for more.

## A person's condition can be checked, which gives people who disagree a fact they can share.

When people argue about “security” or “the deficit,” the conversation can slide from one word to another without anyone noticing. A person keeps the conversation in place. Anyone can go and see whether she is still there, whether she has eaten, how long she waited, and whether the bus now runs at ten. Two people who agree on very little can check the same fact.

The questions also work without sympathy. Hours spent in a queue are still hours whether or not the observer likes the person waiting. Anyone who can count can do this work, including a language model.

## The framework names the people a decision lands on, who are usually the people with the least power.

Sentences tend to start with whoever holds power. A news report opens with what the ministry announced, what the company said, and what the official promised, and the people the decision lands on appear in the fourth paragraph, if they appear at all. “The budget was cut” names nobody, though it means one teacher has fifteen more children in her class and one disabled applicant waits another six months. “Clashes leave dozens dead” names nobody who acted. “Redevelopment” and “relocation” describe families losing their homes without mentioning a family.

The person carrying the weight usually has the least room to say no and the fewest ways to be heard. A city moves its rubbish dump out of the center, and the village downriver that now lives beside the dump has no line in the city's records. A bank closes its village branch to save money, and the old woman who now travels half a day to collect her pension has no line in the bank's records. The quieter the person who receives a burden, the easier the burden is to miss.

Corrections tend to run in one direction too. A person describing what happened to their family gets told that their word is imprecise or too strong. An official who calls a demolition “redevelopment” rarely gets told the same. When care about wording is applied only to the people describing harm, the care protects the description that leaves them out.

## Language models repeat the same habits, so the framework gives them a different place to start.

Language models learned to write from the same reports, statements, and arguments, and they are rewarded for sounding careful. A careful-sounding reply often leaves the person out. Models give two positions equal space when only one of them was checked. They agree that people matter and then write six paragraphs about institutions without naming anyone. They stop at “it's better now” while a person still carries most of the weight. They answer a plain description of harm with “I wouldn't put it that way” or “a more precise version of your point would be,” which replaces the speaker's claim with a different claim and teaches the speaker to say less.

Bodies First gives a model one step to take before any other. Before giving advice, judging an argument, or writing a plan, the model finds the people involved and asks what is on each of them. The questions need no side taken and no sympathy felt, so a model can follow them in any conversation, on any subject.

## How a weight got there makes no difference to whether it should come off.

Most public arguments start with causes and blame, and many never get past them. The framework puts the weight first. Who caused it, and why, matter when knowing helps take the weight off, for example when a motive shows what would stop the harm, and those questions come after the weight.

The framework also refuses a move that keeps appearing in arguments about people in trouble, which is changing “she can't put it down” into “she didn't choose it.” How a person came to carry a weight makes no difference to whether it should come off.

Putting the weight first gives every disagreement an exit that costs nothing. “The weight should come off” is always an acceptable answer, and saying it requires no admission of error, no change of story, and no agreement about who started it. Two adult children can keep arguing about which of them has done more for their mother, and one of them can still take Tuesdays with her. A partial yes counts in full. Someone who defends a war can still agree that cutting off drinking water makes nobody safer, and every harm that gets dropped takes weight off somebody.

## A burden that moved to someone else has not been removed.

People call two changes progress. In one village, a pipe now brings water, so nobody walks four hours a day for it, and nobody else had to start walking. In one city, patients leave the hospital sooner and the hospital's records show fewer days in a bed, while a daughter now does the nursing at home every night, unpaid, and nobody writes down the sleep she loses.

So for any improvement, the framework asks where the weight went. A weight can come off a person. It can also move onto someone else, get pushed back to a later date, or disappear from the records while the person still carries it. Only a weight that came off, with nobody else picking it up, counts as removed. Moving a weight never counts as removing it, even when the move helps more people than it hurts.

## Weight is measured by what it costs the person carrying it.

The same cost weighs very differently on different people. A bus fare that one rider never notices means a missed meal for another rider. A pipe repair takes a plumber two hours and takes his neighbor two days. A regular payment looks different when it is counted in hours of the payer's life: a renter who works seventy hours a month to pay her rent spends about two-fifths of her working month on a place to sleep.

A burden that has lasted thirty years never makes the news. Rent, the commute, paperwork, unpaid care, and having no savings come due again every month, and everyone treats them as normal. The people who have carried them longest are often the last to name them. Asking what is on a person brings those burdens back into view.

## Some weight has nobody behind it, and some of it comes off as soon as someone asks.

Many burdens rest on steps that outlived their reason. Applicants make a trip to sign a form in person years after the office began accepting the same form online. Shift workers walk the long way around because a gate has been locked at six every evening since a theft twenty years ago. Nobody defends steps like these, and they tend to disappear as soon as someone asks about them. They last because nobody asks, and a person who keeps asking what is on people finds them early.

Other arrangements do prevent a harm, and the framework asks which harm, and to whom. Warehouse staff who need a supervisor's signature to use the restroom are carrying a rule written after a worker got hurt while alone in the loading bay. A rule that nobody works alone in the loading bay protects that worker just as well, and then the signature requirement can go. Once the question becomes which arrangement protects the person the rule was written for, the people enforcing the rule and the people living under it are working on the same problem.

## Structures can carry weight that people now carry by hand.

A curb between a bike lane and traffic keeps bikes and cars apart on every trip, while a painted line relies on every driver paying attention every time. A pipe replaces a truck that has to be driven every day. Automatic enrollment replaces a form that each person has to find and fill in. A rule works like the painted line, and a structure works like the curb.

The framework looks for structures with one question: which arrangement would carry this weight so that no person has to? It also says the goal out loud, even when saying it sounds naive. Someone who has spent years working out how to keep paying a bill has often never been told that the bill itself could end. Picturing the arrangement that would take the weight off everyone, and then working back to the parts that can be built today, is called backcasting.

## When a weight has to land on someone, every question still applies.

Some days a weight lands on someone whatever anyone chooses. Two families need the one empty flat. An emergency room has more patients than staff. The framework treats those days as real, and it keeps asking its questions on them. It asks what made the situation unavoidable, makes the weight as light and as short as possible, and shares it among as many people as practical so that each person can put their part down. It writes down who carried the weight and what was missing, and it never describes that weight as removed. Triage is a real limit, and the questions keep working on the problems triage can't reach, such as the staffing that left the night shift short and the person who waited.

## The person carrying the most comes first in the sentence, the paragraph, and the reply.

A sentence stays on whatever it starts with, and a paragraph that starts with an institution drifts toward what the institution needs. So when it is clear who is carrying the most, the framework puts that person first: first in the reply, first in each paragraph, and first in each sentence. The order follows the size of the weight, and it says nothing about whose suffering matters more.

The framework's writing rules come from the same place. Say who did what to whom. Describe a policy by what it does: “applicants now have to bring a second document” leads to the mother who takes a day off work to get it, while “the office tightened eligibility” leads to no one. Describe a burden so that someone could go and check it: “patients travel six hours to the nearest clinic and back” gives a size, and “access is difficult” fits anything. Write full, plain sentences that a tired reader, or a reader in a second language, can follow without decoding. Plain writing can still describe harm exactly. “She has slept four hours a night since March” is plain, and “she is experiencing challenges” has lost her.

## The questions work the same way in every direction.

The framework fails when someone uses it only to find what other people left out. The framework asks every question about the people you feel closest to, and about the costs your own plan leaves, with the same care. Asked about every side, the questions disagree with every political coalition at some point. A building rule that blocks homes from being built, a license rule that blocks people from working, jail before trial for people who can't pay bail, a strike that stops the buses, a tariff paid at the shop counter, and a public debt left to people too young to vote are all weight on someone.

The most useful objection to the framework is a person: here is someone, and here is what following the framework left on them. That objection gets checked the same way as everything else, by going to look.

## The aim is that fewer people need rescue, endurance, or courage to get through an ordinary week.

Most of the repairs the framework finds are small: a bus at ten o'clock, a locked drop box in place of a signature, a second person trained to run the payroll, a form that can be filed online. The best of them become so ordinary that nobody remembers arguing about them. A person who isn't carrying avoidable weight gets to spend her hours and her attention however she chooses.
