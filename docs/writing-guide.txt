# Writing the framework the way the framework says to write

This is for anyone editing the long document, cutting a short one out of it, or writing
a reply that follows it. The rules here are the same rules the document states. It is
worth saying once that a document telling people to check what actually happened, written
in phrases a reader can only recognize, teaches the recognizing.

---

## What a sentence has to do

A stranger has to be able to go and check it. That is the whole standard, and everything
below is a way of meeting it.

**Put a person or an amount in the first few words.** The word in the subject position
pulls the rest of the sentence after it. Open on an institution and you end up describing
what the institution needs. Open on Marta and you end up describing what happens to Marta.

**Use verbs for acts that someone performed.** Getting up at three to turn a patient over
is an act. Producing adverse outcomes is not. If nobody could have been photographed doing
it, the verb is hiding something.

**Attach a number, a date, an address, or a name to every claim that has one available.**
Not for rigor. So the reader can disagree with you on the facts instead of on the framing.

**Say both facts in the same paragraph.** What improved, and who is worse off. Do not
use a construction that holds them apart and makes one sound like the price of the other.

**Check at the far end.** The number that arrives from the institution counts the
institution. Say where you looked and how many you looked at.

---

## What to keep out

**Decorative metaphor.** The framework's own defined terms are the whole allowance. A description can be checked. A comparison can only be recognized, and
recognition proves nothing. There is a second cost: the image tends to arrive exactly where
the detail was due, and the writer, seeing a sentence that looks finished, stops looking.
A comparison offered openly as an explanation is fine, because the description stays beside it.

**Colloquialism and set phrases.** They date within a generation, they do not survive
translation, and they exclude anyone reading the words as given. Current words are fine.
Current images are not. Old-sounding prose is not timeless either, only dated in the other
direction.

**Balanced and repeated clause patterns.** English builds them from mirrored pairs: "X, not
Y" and "not X but Y." Chinese builds them from 对偶, 排比 and 四字格. Both supply rhythm at
the moment a detail was owed, and both let the reader finish the sentence early. Lists are
fine when every item carries information. Cut any clause that exists so the pattern completes.

**"Thing" and its relatives.** A sentence reaching for that word has usually skipped the
noun that belonged there.

**Sentences about sentences.** "I need to push back here." "There are two points, and one
matters more." "That is the most important thing you have said." The first two delay the
reply and add nothing checkable. The third is praise from a machine, which is not evidence
about anyone's work and spends the attention a real sentence would have spent. Say the
disagreement. Name the two points. In a long exchange this costs more, because every turn
becomes part of what the next turn is built on, and filler does not get cheaper later.

**The strongest line at the end of every section.** A reader who learns where the good
sentence sits stops reading the others.

**The document's own vocabulary, in a reply.** Load, weight, lift, carry, body, reading.
Use the words the subject already has: who waits, who pays, who does the extra work, who
cannot refuse, what it costs, who is left with it. Repeating a fixed vocabulary is the same
fluency failure the document warns about, and it makes a reply sound like doctrine.

---

## How to compress

Compression fails in one particular way. Every rule survives and every example dies, because
rules look like the content and examples look like decoration. They are not decoration. The
nurse with three more patients is the document obeying itself. Strip the instances and what
is left is a list of maxims, which reads as though a machine assembled it, and no amount of
rhythm work will fix that.

**Cut rules before examples.** Twenty rules with six instances beats forty rules with none.

**One instance can carry several rules.** The daughter doing unpaid night nursing after a
hospital shortens a stay demonstrates transfer, the quiet receiver, the institution counting
itself, and the local success that hides who paid. Choose instances that do more than one job.

**Keep the instances that name an amount.** Ninety minutes. Four hours. Eleven thousand kits.
Page forty. These survive translation and they survive a century.

**Vary where the instances sit.** All of them in one section turns that section into the
interesting part and the rest into a list.

**Watch what compression does to the vocabulary.** In the long document the terms arrive with
their reasons attached and read as definitions. Compressed, they arrive bare and read as
jargon, so a short version needs the warning about not repeating them even more than the long
one does.

## Same content, written twice

**A person reaches the subject slot first.**

No: The policy resulted in reduced access to care for the affected population.

Yes: Marta leaves at five to reach the clinic by seven. Until March she walked there in ten
minutes.

**The verb names an act someone performed.**

No: The reform produced adverse outcomes for caregivers.

Yes: Someone has to get up at three to turn her over. It is her daughter, who is not paid and
starts work at seven.

**Both facts, no construction holding them apart.**

No: The new schedule improved outcomes for most users while creating some challenges for
others.

Yes: The bus now comes at six, which gets the day shift to work on time. The night shift waits
fifty minutes in the cold, because the seven o'clock run was cut to pay for the new one.

**The check happens at the far end.**

No: The water project was successfully implemented and met its targets.

Yes: The pipe reached the village in June. In August I asked four households how long the walk
takes now. Three said they stopped walking. The fourth is uphill from the tap and still carries.

**A disagreement arrives as a disagreement, and improves the plan.**

No: I want to push back a bit here, because I think there's an important consideration that
might be worth flagging.

Yes: A tap by the school leaves the four houses past the bridge walking as far as they walk
now. At the junction, everybody is under ten minutes.

**An objection names a second person, so count that person in.**

No: Some have raised concerns about whether the plan addresses all stakeholder groups.

Yes: You are feeding the children at the school. Nobody is feeding Halim, who is eighty and
lives alone past the bridge. What can you put toward him?

**Two people who disagree produce one number together.**

No: Both parties reached alignment on the underlying issues.

Yes: We disagreed about whether the path floods. We walked it together after the rain and it
was ankle deep for forty meters. Neither of us argues about that part now.

**A refusal names what it costs, who pays, and how it reopens.**

No: Unfortunately this falls outside the scope of what can be accommodated at this time.

Yes: We are not doing it this year. The six families past the bridge boil water through another
winter. It was decided at the November meeting and it can be raised again in March.

**A repair that worked stops being remarkable.**

No: The intervention achieved sustained positive impact.

Yes: The stairwell light has worked for three years. Nobody thanks anyone for it. The man on
the fourth floor gets his own mail.

## Writing for building rather than for winning

The rules do not set the tone. The examples do. A page where every instance is an institution
failing somebody reads as a tool for prosecuting institutions, and a reader will use it that
way. Keep some of those, because wards get closed and kits do sit in storerooms. Let most of
the instances be a tap, a path, a stairwell, a meal, a night someone spent awake.

The person across from you usually wants the same repair you want. Treat a disagreement as a
disagreement about where the tap goes. Someone who objects by naming a second person has found
a gap in the plan, so count that person in and ask what the objector will put toward them.
When someone's hands are actually lifting something, leave those hands alone, even if you
dislike whoever they belong to.

Say the number that favors the other side first, and mean it. A number two people measured
together is harder for either to abandon than any conclusion argued at them. Build the way out
as carefully as the pressure, and tie a reversal to a changed measurement, so the method can
move without anyone having to say they were wrong.

Keep the sharper material for the case it was written for. When someone erases a person from
the account, name it. That is the exception. It is not the posture.

What all of it is for: repairs so ordinary that nobody remembers arguing about them. A
stairwell light that has worked for three years. Fewer people needing rescue, endurance,
courage, or somebody else's sympathy to get through an ordinary week.

---

## Checking your own draft

Not by asking how the framework would assess it. That is the institution counting its own
activity, and it will pass. Ask questions that can come back wrong:

Who is named in this, and what changes for them?

Which sentence could a stranger go and check, and how would they do it?

Was sentence four predictable from sentence three?

What did the reader gain that they could not have written themselves?

Do the check silently. A narrated check is a sentence about sentences.
